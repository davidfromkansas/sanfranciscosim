
# Stylized Miniature Tech-City Architecture

## Purpose
Use this skill when creating buildings, campuses, streets, landmarks, infrastructure, or other structures in a stylized miniature city inspired by premium animated architectural dioramas and the visual language of the Silicon Valley title sequence.

The goal is not photorealism and not generic low-poly art. The target is a believable city compressed into a charming, highly legible tabletop world: recognizable architecture simplified into bold forms, clean materials, oversized semantic details, and playful environmental storytelling.

The core principle is: **Real architecture, compressed into charming miniature landmarks.**

Every design decision should optimize for recognition, readability, personality, and composition from an elevated city-scale camera rather than literal physical accuracy.

## 1. Core Visual Identity
The world should feel like a hybrid of: a handcrafted architectural scale model · a premium animated 3D diorama · an isometric city-building or strategy game · a model railroad with dense environmental storytelling · tilt-shift photography of a real city · a playful but sophisticated visualization of a real place.

Buildings should be recognizable as plausible architecture, but they should look designed and art-directed, not scanned from reality. The viewer should immediately understand what a structure is from its silhouette, massing, color, signage, and a few exaggerated features.

Aim for: miniature · tactile · clean · playful · polished · dense · colorful · architectural · legible · charming · slightly exaggerated · physically grounded · visually coherent.

Avoid: photorealism · gritty realism · generic low-poly assets · voxel aesthetics · flat cartoon buildings · excessive tiny geometry · noisy procedural textures · realistic dirt and decay unless narratively necessary · cyberpunk visual clutter · extreme sci-fi styling unless the subject specifically calls for it.

## 2. Design Hierarchy
Prioritize in this order: 1 Silhouette · 2 Overall massing · 3 Iconic architectural feature · 4 Identity/signage · 5 Major facade rhythm · 6 Roof treatment · 7 Entrances and plazas · 8 Landscaping · 9 Secondary architectural details · 10 Tiny decorative details.

If a detail is technically accurate but makes the building harder to read from the intended camera, simplify or remove it. A successful structure should remain recognizable at thumbnail size.

## 3. Massing and Proportions
Construct from simple, confident geometric volumes: rectangular cuboids, stacked slabs, cylinders, wedges, extruded polygons, simple arches, broad curved walls, low platforms, stepped terraces, occasional dramatic sculptural forms. Use large shapes first — a complex real building might become one main volume + one offset secondary volume + a recessed entrance + three window bands + one rooftop landmark.

Favor: chunky floor slabs · substantial roof thickness · slightly oversized windows and entrances · broad readable setbacks · compact footprints · strong separation between major volumes. Important structures may be 10–25% larger than strict realism if that improves city-scale readability. Scale should be semantically correct rather than mathematically perfect.

## 4. Geometry
Clean and economical without appearing primitive. Use broad planar surfaces, controlled bevels on exposed edges, shallow facade recesses, simple window extrusions or inset planes, clean roof parapets, clearly separated volumes, smooth curves where they create a landmark silhouette. Avoid microscopic facade geometry, individually modeled bolts/vents/mullions/bricks, topology invisible from the target camera, visibly faceted curves (unless intentional), razor-sharp CG edges everywhere.

**Beveling:** subtle bevels are important — they catch highlights and make structures resemble physical miniatures. Keep bevels visually consistent across the environment.

## 5. Facade Language
Communicate architecture through rhythm, not detail. Systems: **horizontal office bands** (light slab / dark window strip, repeated), **window grid** (regular simplified windows between broad structure), **curtain wall** (broad tinted surface, restrained mullions), **solid institutional facade** (large cream/white/gray surfaces with a handful of deep readable openings).

**Window philosophy:** windows are graphical elements before they are literal openings — dark blue-gray, desaturated cyan-gray, or charcoal-blue; slightly reflective; relatively opaque; regular; grouped into clear bands or grids; subtly recessed. No interiors, desks, blinds, or rooms at city scale.

## 6. Materials
Evoke painted architectural miniatures: painted resin, foam-core, molded plastic, matte acrylic. Concrete/plaster: smooth, subtle roughness, warm white/ivory/beige/pale-to-medium gray. Glass: tinted, dark, moderately reflective, simplified, consistent. Metal: clean, slightly matte; reserve high reflectivity for focal accents. Roofs: clean and graphically readable. **Weathering: minimal by default** — no dirt streaks, rust, cracks, moss, stains, or grime unless they communicate something important.

## 7. Color System
**Neutral architecture + saturated identity + saturated environmental accents.** Architectural base: warm white, cream, beige, pale gray, medium gray, charcoal, muted blue-gray glass. Saturated accents reserved for branding, vehicles, umbrellas, sculptures, transit, public furniture, construction equipment, special features. Vegetation may be unusually vivid — clean saturated green. Avoid making every building equally colorful; the world needs neutral visual resting areas.

## 8. Branding and Semantic Exaggeration
Identity is architecture. Signage may be dramatically oversized: giant rooftop lettering, freestanding logo sculptures, facade wordmarks, sculptural icons, oversized entrance signs. Ask: what visual device lets a viewer identify this place in under one second from an aerial camera? Exaggerate meaningful identifiers, never randomly.

## 9. Semantic Scale
Selectively enlarge for readability: signs, logos, people, cars, buses, trees, windows, doors, rooftop equipment, solar panels, road markings, traffic lights, sculptures, furniture — like cartography. Rule: if an object disappears at the intended camera distance but carries meaning, enlarge or simplify it. Don't let exaggeration destroy the coherent-city illusion.

## 10. Roofs as Secondary Facades
The camera looks down — roofs are major surfaces. Never leave prominent roofs blank. Features: solar arrays, HVAC masses, skylights, terraces, gardens, seating, helipads, antennas, water tanks, sculptures, logos, small gatherings, mechanical penthouses, geometric patterns. Organize into clear clusters, not scattered props. Solar panels give strong graphical repetition.

## 11. Landmark Design
Roughly **80% simple architectural massing / 20% distinctive landmark geometry** (circular buildings, sweeping roofs, towers, oversized arches, cantilevers, domes, unusual rooftop sculptures). If everything is unusual, nothing feels special.

## 12. Landscaping
Simplified, sculptural, intentionally composed. Trees: simple trunks, chunky crowns, grouped foliage volumes, readable silhouettes; differentiate species via silhouette/height/proportion/color, not leaves. Place to frame architecture: street rows, plaza clusters, courtyards, geometric lawns, medians, rooftop gardens. Manicured unless wilderness is required.

## 13. Roads and Ground Plane
Roads are major compositional shapes: clean charcoal asphalt, bright readable markings, simplified intersections, broad sidewalks, crisp curbs, occasional sweeping curves, traffic islands, landscaped medians, plazas. Curved roads soften rectangular cities. Break up any large empty asphalt with trees, vehicles, markings, medians, planters, activity, small structures.

## 14. Vehicles and Street Objects
Colorful scale cues: simplified, slightly chunky, immediately recognizable, cleanly colored, slightly oversized when necessary. No interiors. Props (buses, cars, vans, bikes, construction vehicles, signals, benches, umbrellas, bollards, kiosks) imply activity — don't reproduce every real object.

## 15. People
Scale and life. At aerial distance: silhouette, pose, clothing color, clustering, activity; may be slightly oversized. Small groups tell micro-stories (rooftop coworkers, crossing pedestrians, outdoor lunches, construction workers, event crowds, cyclists). Create deliberate activity nodes, never uniform distribution.

## 16. Environmental Storytelling
Every important block gets at least one clue something is happening: a crane beside a growing startup, rooftop party, delivery van, lunch tables, maintenance crew, sculpture, parked bikes, traffic queue, small event, helicopter, construction fencing, transit commuters. Reward closer inspection; keep subordinate to the architecture.

## 17. Composition and Density
Real-world spacing may be compressed for stronger relationships, better density, clearer storytelling, fewer dead zones, better compositions. Within each cluster vary height, orientation, footprint, roof treatment, landscaping, signage, facade rhythm. Avoid repetitive rows of interchangeable boxes.

## 18. Camera Assumptions
Design primarily for a high three-quarter aerial view: 30–50° downward, longer focal length, restrained perspective, near-isometric compression, substantial city visible. Roofs, silhouettes, horizontal composition, and ground patterns matter; entrances may need exaggeration; tiny facade details matter less. Always inspect from the intended aerial camera — never optimize only from street level.

## 19. Lighting
Bright attractive daylight on a physical miniature: broad soft key, readable directional shadows, substantial ambient fill, subtle ambient occlusion, strong contact shadows, pleasant highlights, clear separation. Avoid harsh darkness, crushed blacks, extreme HDR reflections, neon (unless requested), flat shadowless light. AO especially at foundations, window recesses, roof equipment, trees, overlapping volumes, stairs, plazas.

## 20. Miniature / Tilt-Shift Effect
Evoke macro photography of a scale model: elevated camera, compressed perspective, saturated controlled color, clean geometry, tiny characters, strong contact shadows, selective depth of field. The miniature effect should come primarily from design language and scale cues, not a blur filter.

## 21. Detail Budget
Hero landmark: distinctive massing, richer facade articulation, branded architecture, detailed roof, landscaping, several storytelling props. Secondary building: clear massing, facade rhythm, simple roof, one or two identity cues. Background building: silhouette, broad facade pattern, roof color/value, perhaps one feature. Never spend geometry on features occupying a few pixels.

## 22. Converting a Real Building
1 Identify the 3–5 recognition cues. 2 Remove nonessential information. 3 Rebuild massing from few clean volumes. 4 Exaggerate the signature feature. 5 Simplify the facade into bands/grids/panels. 6 Design the roof. 7 Add identity (signage, color, landscaping, object). 8 Ground it (sidewalk, access, landscaping, people, props). 9 Evaluate from the aerial camera (recognizable? distinct silhouette? visible details? noise? same world?). 10 **Simplify again** — the first pass is usually too detailed.

## 23. Creating an Original Building
Start from an archetype (HQ, startup office, civic, lab, apartment tower, warehouse, transit station, museum, hospital, factory, retail, data center) and assign ONE dominant personality, describable in one sentence. Don't combine every interesting idea into one building.

## 24. Structure Families
Create families, not duplicates: share material palette, window language, slab thickness, roof style, landscaping vocabulary; vary height, footprint, orientation, setback, accent feature, signage. Coherence without repetition.

## 25. Infrastructure
Same principles. Bridges: bold deck, clear supports, iconic structural silhouette. Transit: canopy, platform, signage, movement. Construction sites: oversized cranes, exposed grids, bright machinery. Utility structures: function legible via one or two recognizable features. All part of the same handcrafted miniature kit.

## 26. What "Stylized" Means Here
Deliberate compression of reality, not arbitrary cartoon distortion: 40 real windows → 8 strong bands; complicated mechanical roof → 3 clean HVAC blocks; subtle sign → giant readable logo; dozens of tree species → 4 sculptural families; huge parking lot → smaller graphic parking. Every simplification should make the environment easier to understand.

## 27. Common Failure Modes
Generic low-poly (faceted, random colors, no rhythm) → use believable massing, clean surfaces, restrained materials, recognizable facade systems. Photorealism (PBR noise, grime, tiny details) → simplify materials, enlarge forms. Every building a box → controlled variation in footprint/setback/roofline/orientation + landmark geometry. Every building a landmark → restore hierarchy. Blank rooftops → design them. Tiny realistic signs → semantic scale. Dead city → clusters of people, vehicles, landscaping, construction, activity. Childishly toy-like → maintain architectural credibility; sophisticated proportions, lighting, materials. Too much detail → judge at final camera distance.

## 28. Quality Checklist
Silhouette reads from the aerial camera · understandable at thumbnail scale · few strong forms · simplified but plausible · subtle bevels · clean readable facade patterns · simplified windows, no interiors · clean miniature-like materials · restrained architecture + intentional saturated accents · identity visible at distance · roof intentionally designed · landscaping frames the architecture · props and people provide scale · at least one environmental story near important structures · semantic exaggeration where needed · distinct from neighbors · still belongs to the same visual world · no detail exists solely because the reference had it · feels like a premium physical miniature brought to life.

## 29. Agent Decision Rules
Realism vs readability → readability. Detail vs silhouette → silhouette. Exact scale vs recognition → semantic scale. Texture vs geometry/broad color → the cleaner solution. Roof visible → design it. Lacks personality → strengthen ONE defining characteristic. Looks generic → identify the archetype and exaggerate one cue. Feels sterile → purposeful human activity, not clutter. Looks photoreal → simplify materials. Looks childish → restore architectural logic. Looks generically low-poly → increase architectural specificity, not polygons.

## 30. Compact Generation Directive
"Create the structure as a premium stylized miniature architectural model viewed from a high three-quarter city-scale camera. Preserve believable architectural massing and the subject's most recognizable features, but simplify reality into clean chunky geometric volumes, subtle bevels, broad facade rhythms, dark blue-gray windows, smooth matte materials, and intentionally designed rooftops. Use restrained white, cream, beige, gray, and charcoal architecture with saturated branding, landscaping, vehicles, and environmental accents. Exaggerate semantically important elements such as logos, entrances, windows, people, trees, and rooftop features so they remain readable from a distance. Surround the structure with manicured miniature landscaping, clean roads and sidewalks, small clusters of people, vehicles, and one or two playful storytelling details. Favor recognition and visual clarity over literal scale or microscopic accuracy. The finished result should feel like a sophisticated handcrafted tabletop city diorama brought to life — not photorealistic, not voxel art, and not generic low-poly."

## 31. North Star
"I recognize that place immediately — but it looks like someone built a beautiful miniature version of it for a living tabletop city."

## Project reconciliation notes (SF-specific)
- **SF housing exception to §7:** painted Victorian/Sunset rows ARE saturated identity in San Francisco — tinted facades stay (via the tintable `Toy_body` system), but trim, roofs, and streets remain restrained so rows read as accents against a calm base.
- **Voxel caution:** §1 "avoid voxel aesthetics" applies to buildings and props; the Alcatraz heightfield terrain is grandfathered pending David's review — prefer smoother terraced forms for new terrain.
- The asset contract (flat colors, `Toy_*` materials, `_Glow`, base-center origin, meters) and `/sf-asset-check` remain the technical gate; this skill is the artistic gate. Both must pass.
